Remembering: The Children on Charlie忆念：晚辈谈芒格

From Charles T. Munger Jr.

小查尔斯·T·芒格 撰

On the last day of a family ski vacation in Sun Valley when I was 15 or so, my dad and I were driving back in the snow when he took a 10-minute detour to gas the red Jeep33Red Jeep红色吉普车 we were driving. He was pressed for time to have our family catch the plane home, so I was surprised to notice as he pulled into the station that the tank was still half full. I asked my dad why we had stopped when we had plenty of gas, and he admonished me, “Charlie, when you borrow a man’s car, you always return it with a full tank of gas.”

在我大约15岁的时候，全家去太阳谷（Sun Valley）滑雪度假，假期的最后一天，爸爸和我冒着大雪开车返程，他特意绕了十分钟的路，去给我们开的那辆红色吉普车加油。当时他正赶着让全家赶上回家的飞机，时间很紧，所以我挺意外——开进加油站时我注意到油箱还是半满的。我问爸爸，既然油还够，为什么要停下来；他训诫我说：“查理（Charlie），你借了别人的车，还回去的时候油箱一定要是满的。”

My freshman year at Stanford, an acquaintance lent me his car, more because friends we had in common twisted his arm than because he knew me all that well. The tank was half full, and the Audi Fox was red. So I remembered the Jeep and topped up the tank before I brought the car back. He noticed. We’ve had a lot of good times since, and he stood as a groomsman at my wedding.

我读斯坦福（Stanford）大一那年，一个熟人把车借给我——与其说他跟我多熟，不如说是我们共同的朋友硬劝他这么做的。油箱里还剩一半油，那辆奥迪 Fox（Audi Fox）是红色的。于是我想起了那辆吉普车，还车前先把油箱加满了。他注意到了。从那以后我们共度过许多美好时光，我结婚时他还做了我的伴郎。

After Stanford, I learned that on that vacation we had been staying at Rick Guerin’s34Rick Guerin里克·格林 house and driving Rick Guerin’s Jeep. Rick is one of my dad’s friends who, on his return to Sun Valley, certainly wouldn’t have been troubled, and was unlikely even to notice, if his Jeep had had less gas than when he left it. My dad still didn’t skip a point of fairness and consideration. So I was taught that day not only how to get a good friend but also how to keep one.

从斯坦福毕业后我才知道，那次度假我们住的是瑞克·格伦（Rick Guerin）的家，开的是瑞克·格伦的吉普车。瑞克是爸爸的朋友，等他回到太阳谷，就算吉普车的油比他离开时少了，他也肯定不会介意，甚至多半根本不会察觉。可爸爸一点没马虎，照样把公平和体贴做到位。所以那天我学到的，不单是怎么交到好朋友，还有怎么留住好朋友。

“I was taught that day not only how to get a good friend but also how to keep one.”那一天我学到的，不只是如何交到好朋友，还有如何留住好朋友。

From Wendy Munger

温迪·芒格 撰

My dad often used the forum of the family dinner table to try to educate his children.36Educating his children教育子女 His favorite educational tools were the Morality Tale, in which someone faced an ethical problem and chose the correct path, and the Downward Spiral Tale,37Downward Spiral下行螺旋 in which someone made the wrong choice and suffered an inevitable series of catastrophic personal and professional losses.

爸爸常常利用家庭餐桌这个讲坛来教育我们这些孩子。他最钟爱的教育工具是两类故事：一类是“德育故事”，讲某人面对道德难题，选择了正确道路；另一类是“下行螺旋故事”，讲某人做了错误选择，随之不可避免地遭遇一连串生活与事业上的惨重损失。

His specialty was the Downward Spiral Tale. He could really warm to the topic of apocalyptic consequences. He used such extreme and horrific examples that we were often simultaneously groaning and laughing by the time he finished. He’s in a league of his own when it comes to describing negative outcomes and the lessons to be learned from them.

他最拿手的是“下行螺旋故事”。讲到那些世界末日般的后果，他总能越讲越来劲。他举的例子极端又吓人，往往讲到最后，我们一边哀叹一边大笑。论起描绘糟糕结局、以及从中该吸取的教训，他可谓独步天下。

“He used such extreme and horrific examples that we were often simultaneously groaning and laughing by the time he finished.”他用的例子如此极端、骇人，以至于讲完时，我们常常一边呻吟一边大笑。

His Morality Tales were more straightforward. I remember the story my dad told his kids, then ranging from age 5 to 25, about a financial officer at one of his companies who made a mistake that resulted in the loss of hundreds of thousands of dollars to the company. As soon as this officer realized his mistake, he went to the president of the company and told him about it. My dad told us that the president then said, “This was a terrible mistake, and we don’t want you ever to make another one like it. But people make mistakes, and we can forgive that. You did the right thing, which was to admit your mistake. If you had tried to hide the mistake, or cover it up for even a short time, you would be out of this company. As it is, we’d like you to stay.” I think back on this story every time I hear of yet another government official who chose the cover-up instead of the honest confession after making a mistake.

他的“德育故事”则直白得多。我记得爸爸讲给孩子们听的那个故事——当时我们兄弟姐妹年纪从5岁到25岁不等——说的是他旗下一家公司里，有位财务主管犯了错，让公司损失了几十万美元。这位主管一发现自己出错，立刻去找公司总裁坦白了。爸爸告诉我们，总裁当时说：“这是个严重的错误，我们不希望你再犯同样的错。但人总会犯错，这一点我们可以原谅。你做对了的事，就是承认了错误。要是你试图隐瞒，哪怕只遮掩一小会儿，你就会被公司开除。既然如此，我们希望你留下来。”此后每当我听说又有哪位政府官员犯了错不去诚实坦白、反而选择掩盖，就会想起这个故事。

I don’t know why I use the past tense in describing my dad’s educational efforts at the dinner table. His oldest children are heading toward their 60s, the table is now crowded with grandchildren, and he’s still using his distinctive style of storytelling to keep us on the side of the angels. We’re very lucky to have him at the head of our table.

我不知道自己为什么在描述爸爸餐桌上的教育时用了过去时。他最大的孩子都快六十了，餐桌上现在挤满了孙辈，可他仍用他那独特的讲故事方式，把我们引向天使那一边。能有他坐在餐桌的主位上，我们何其幸运。

“The table is now crowded with grandchildren, and he’s still using his distinctive style of storytelling to keep us on the side of the angels.”如今餐桌坐满了孙辈，而他仍用那独特的说故事风格，把我们留在天使的这一边。

From William H. (Hal) Borthwick

威廉·H·（哈尔）·博思威克 撰

It has been a fascinating and wonderful 50 years (nearly) since Charlie and my mother were married. There were many opportunities that I offered Charlie for formative education. Here are a couple:

查理（Charlie）和妈妈结婚，到如今已将近五十年，这段时光迷人而美好。我曾给过查理许多让我受教的机会。下面说两件：

Do the job right the first time

第一次就把事情做对

This story goes back to Minnesota times. One of my jobs as a driving-age teen was to pick up and deliver the housekeeper from the town of Cass Lake. This wasn’t just a drive down the street; the boat had to be driven across the lake to the marina,38Marina游艇码头 where I would hop into the car to drive to town, and then the process was reversed. Part of my job in the morning was to pick up a newspaper while I was in town.

这个故事要回溯到明尼苏达州的那些日子。我到了能开车的年纪时，有一项任务是去卡斯湖（Cass Lake）镇接女管家并送她回家。这可不只是在街上开段路——得先把船开过湖到码头，我再跳上车开进镇子，回来时原路倒一遍。我早晨的差事里，还包括在镇上买份报纸。

Well, one day a big storm39Big storm大风暴 blew in: rain, waves, wind, etc., big time. With all the excitement and difficulty, I did get to town in the morning and returned with the maid, but I forgot the paper. Charlie and I had a one-second or so discussion after I answered the question “Where’s my paper?” in the negative. “Go back and get the paper and never forget it again!” So back I went through the storm40Through the storm穿越风暴 to get the paper, bouncing in the waves with rain sheeting off the boat, thinking to myself that I wasn’t going to allow anything like this ever to happen again.

有一天，一场大风暴袭来——大雨、巨浪、狂风，样样都来势汹汹。千难万险之中，我总算早上到了镇上，把女佣接了回来，却把报纸忘了。查理问我“我的报纸呢？”，我答没买，于是我俩大约有那么一秒钟的商量。“回去把报纸拿来，以后别再忘了！”于是我冒着风暴原路返回去取报纸，小船在浪里颠簸，雨水在船上横飞，我心里想着：绝不让这种事再发生第二次。

Be responsible

勇于担当

Charlie’s mother drove herself from Omaha to Minnesota each summer. When she was there, we used her car for errands. There was but one set of keys, and while I was playing with friends in a sailing boat on the lake, the keys fell out of my pocket into 5 feet of murky water. I went home and confessed. Of course, in the Great North Woods, there aren’t many locksmiths, and with Charlie, there wasn’t patience for such stupidity. The solution, again in about a second, was: “Go out with your friends and keep diving till you get those keys, and don’t come home without them.” After about two hours of diving, with the sun sinking like a stone, the miraculous glint of metal in the weeds was before my eyes, and I could go home.

查理的母亲每年夏天都自己从奥马哈（Omaha）开车来明尼苏达。她在这儿的时候，我们有事就用她的车。可钥匙只有一套，有一次我和朋友在湖上的帆船里玩，钥匙从口袋掉进了五英尺深的浑水。我回家坦白了。当然，在那大北树林里，锁匠没几个，而查理对这种蠢事可没半点耐心。同样大约一秒钟，办法就出来了：“和你朋友出去，一直潜水找到那些钥匙，找不到就别回家。”潜了大约两个小时，太阳像石头一样往下沉，水草丛里忽然闪过一道金属的微光——就在我眼前，这下我能回家了。

There are a lot of these gems from Minnesota because, in those days when Charlie worked so hard and so long, that was the only meaningful time we spent with him. During the work weeks, he was off before dawn and home about dinner time, and then studied Standard & Poor’s and, later, would spend a couple of hours on the phone with Warren.

明尼苏达留下了许多这样的珍宝般的小故事，因为那些年查理工作又拼又久，那是我们能真正和他相处的仅有的宝贵时光。上班的日子里，他天不亮就出门，快吃晚饭才回来，然后研读标准普尔（Standard & Poor's），后来还会花两三个小时和沃伦·巴菲特（Warren Buffett）通电话。

From David Borthwick

戴维·博思威克 撰

Many years ago, Father decided our Minnesota lake cabin absolutely had to have a tennis ball practice41Tennis practice网球练习 machine for the court that had been built a few years earlier. While he certainly wanted the children to groove their groundstrokes, there was a bit more to it than that. For it was Father who was out on the court more than anyone, with the machine positioned so he could endlessly practice-volley close by the net. Before long, he mastered the well-placed easy put-away volleys, the kinds of shots everyone else instinctively tried to kill but usually hit into the net or 10 feet out. By working on the tennis version of golf’s short game, which few others could be bothered to practice, Father, as he’s done throughout his life, gave himself a fair if maddening competitive advantage. I really dreaded playing against him, especially in doubles, where the net play really counts. Thank god it was tennis, not business.

许多年前，父亲认定我们在明尼苏达湖边的木屋绝对得有一台网球发球机，给几年前建好的球场用。他当然希望孩子们把正手抽球练得扎实，可用意不止于此。因为上场最多的其实是父亲——他把机器摆好，好让自己能没完没了地在网前练截击。不久他就练会了那种落点刁钻、轻巧便能得分的截击，这类球别人本能地都想一拍扣死，通常却不是下网就是飞出十英尺开外。靠着练这种相当于高尔夫短杆的网球功课——别人很少愿意费这个劲——父亲一如他毕生所为，给自己谋得了一项虽叫人抓狂却实实在在的竞争优势。我真的很怕跟他打，尤其是双打，因为双打里网前才是关键。谢天谢地，那是网球，不是生意。

Thinking about Father made me remember a long-ago humorous TV beer ad in which a smartly dressed man at a table is so engrossed in his glass of beer as to be oblivious to a rampaging bull charging a bullfighter right in front of him. He doesn’t flinch, even when the bull smashes the table into matchsticks. The announcer’s tagline was “Try this beer for a truly unique experience,” or something like that.

想到父亲，让我记起很久以前一个幽默的电视啤酒广告：一个衣着考究的男人坐在桌前，全神贯注地盯着杯里的啤酒，对眼前一头横冲直撞、正顶向斗牛士的公牛视而不见。哪怕公牛把桌子撞成碎片，他眼皮都不眨一下。旁白的广告词大概是“尝尝这啤酒，体验真正的独一无二”之类。

Take away the beer and substitute the financial market listings, architectural plans, or a scholarly biography of Keynes and you have a dead-on comedic take on Father night after night in his favorite chair42Charlie's favorite chair查理最爱的椅子 poring over something, all but deaf to the roughhousing younger children, blaring TV, and Mom trying to summon him to dinner.

把啤酒拿掉，换成行情报价、建筑图纸，或者一本学术性的凯恩斯（Keynes）传记，你得到的就是父亲夜复一夜的精准喜剧写照：他坐在最爱的椅子上钻研什么，对年幼孩子们的打闹、震耳的电视声，还有妈妈喊他吃饭，几乎充耳不闻。

Even when not reading, Father was often so deep in contemplation that a routine drive to take Molly and Wendy back to Pasadena could have turned into an excursion to San Bernardino without Mom calling out the correct freeway turnoffs. Whatever was on his mind, it wasn’t the outcome of a football game or a botched golf shot. Father’s ability to Chinese wall off the most intrusive distractions from whatever mental task he was engaged in—a practice alternately amusing and irritating if you were trying to get his attention—accounts as much as anything else for his success.

即便没在读书，父亲也常陷入极深的沉思，以至于平常送莫莉（Molly）和温蒂（Wendy）回帕萨迪纳（Pasadena）的车程，要不是妈妈及时喊出正确的高速出口，差点就开到圣贝纳迪诺（San Bernardino）去了。他心里想的绝不是某场橄榄球赛的结果，也不是哪一杆高尔夫打砸了。父亲能把最扰人的干扰像隔着一道墙那样挡在他正琢磨的心事之外——这种本事，要是你正想引起他注意，会叫人又气又好笑——正是这本领，很大程度上成就了他。

“Father was often so deep in contemplation that a routine drive to take Molly and Wendy back to Pasadena could have turned into an excursion to San Bernardino without Mom calling out the correct freeway turnoffs.”父亲常陷入如此深的沉思，以至于一次送莫莉（Molly）和温迪（Wendy）回帕萨迪纳（Pasadena）的寻常车程，若没有母亲喊出正确的高速出口，很可能就开到圣贝纳迪诺（San Bernardino）去了。

Fortune and the Wall Street Journal

From Molly Munger

莫莉·芒格 撰

When I went to college in 1966, I was very lucky to have been thoroughly steeped in Daddy’s influence. In an angry and radical era, I would buy The Wall Street Journal or Fortune at the subway kiosk just outside the college gates, tuck it under my Oxford-cloth arm, and stride off to economics and business classes. People were occupying the dean’s office, going to jail. I was in the basement of the Lamont Library learning how to read a balance sheet.

1966年我上大学时，很幸运早已深深浸染在爸爸的影响之中。在那个愤怒而激进的年代，我会在校门外地铁口的书报亭买一份《华尔街日报》（The Wall Street Journal）或《财富》（Fortune），夹在牛津纺的臂弯里，大步去上经济与商业课。别人正占领院长办公室、进监狱。我却在拉蒙特图书馆（Lamont Library）的地下室学怎么读资产负债表。

“People were occupying the dean’s office, going to jail. I was in the basement of the Lamont Library learning how to read a balance sheet.”别人在占领院长办公室、进监狱。我却在拉蒙特图书馆（Lamont Library）的地下室里，学着怎么读资产负债表。

Daddy raised us to be skeptical, even contrarian, and that was a particularly helpful way of thinking to carry into the maelstrom of the late ʼ60s. Over many years, sitting in the library at our house on June Street, he had told us often funny stories of people who either followed the group too blindly or lashed out too reflexively. “Crazy,” “maladjusted,” “pompous,” “self-satisfied”—we knew from his adjectives what he thought we should avoid.

爸爸把我们教得爱怀疑，甚至逆反，而这种思维方式在六十年代末的动荡漩涡里格外管用。多年来，在我们六月街（June Street）家里的书房，他常讲些有趣的故事，主角要么太盲从大流，要么太冲动失控。“疯狂”、“失调”、“浮夸”、“自满”——从他用的这些形容词里，我们懂了哪些是他要我们避开的。

In Minnesota, he found a way to hard-wire the same message into our very bodies. He had arranged for the old Larson Boat Works to make us an “aquaplane,” a heavy wooden affair we stood on as he towed it behind the boat. He would make sharp turns to see if we could hold on, and the only way to avoid the disgrace of a fall was to keep shifting our weight to compensate for the extreme angles. Then, and on into the future, I would always be viscerally terrified if it seemed any thought or activity was getting out of hand in one direction or another.

在明尼苏达，他找到一种办法，把同样的信息直接“焊”进我们的身体。他请老字号的拉尔森造船厂（Larson Boat Works）给我们做了一块“滑水板”——一块厚木板，他开船拖着，我们站在上面。他会猛地转弯，看我们能不能站稳；要想不掉进水里出丑，唯一的办法就是不停挪动重心，抵消那极大的倾角。从那时起，直到后来，只要任何念头或举动似乎要朝某个方向失控，我就会本能地感到恐惧。

When I was in college, Daddy had seven other children to raise, worked in a seedy part of Spring Street, and owned but one company, a small, grimy outfit that made motor additive. But he saw these were unhinged times. He sent me the allowance of a much richer father, keeping me in professionally ironed shirts and making me feel sharp as a bandbox. From 3,000 miles away, he continued to help me keep my balance.

我上大学时，爸爸还要抚养另外七个孩子，在春天街（Spring Street）一个破败的地段上班，只拥有一家公司——一家又小又脏、生产汽车添加剂的小厂。可他看得出那是段失序的岁月。他按宽裕得多的父亲的标准给我寄生活费，让我穿着专业熨烫的衬衫，觉得自己精神得像刚出厂的新衣。隔着三千英里，他仍在帮我稳住重心。

“From 3,000 miles away, he continued to help me keep my balance.”隔着三千英里，他仍帮我保持平衡。

I could go on. Suffice it to say that our father has always known what he was doing, as a parent as in so much else. I appreciated it greatly. I still do.

我还能接着说下去。只消说一句：无论在为人父还是其他许多方面，父亲向来知道自己做什么。我那时极感激，现在依然。

From Emilie Ogden

埃米莉·奥格登 撰

“You have your father’s hands,” my husband remarked out of the blue, as we shared a glass of wine. I looked at him, a little stunned, not by the comparison but by his telepathy. I had been devising a short piece about my father, and the very subject had been on my mind.

“你的手和你父亲的一样。”我们正共饮一杯葡萄酒时，丈夫冷不丁冒出一句。我看着他，有点怔住——不是因为那比较，而是因为他竟能心灵相通。我当时正在构思一篇写父亲的小文，想的恰恰就是这个。

I had already noticed that my oldest son’s hands are like his grandfather’s, with fingertips slightly square and nail beds shaped like teacups rather than ovals. But it’s something about the way our hands take positions that first sparks the comparison. My father, my son, and I all cross our hands behind us in the same distinct manner, the left hand holding the wrist of the right, as we walk, minds elsewhere.

我早就注意到，大儿子的手像他外公：指尖略方，甲床不是椭圆而是茶杯状。但最先触发这番比较的，是我们手摆放的姿态。父亲、儿子和我，走路时若有所思，都会以同样独特的方式把手背到身后——左手握住右腕。

“What is it about my hands, exactly, that reminds you of my father’s?” I asked.

“到底我手上的什么，让你想起我父亲？”我问。

“It’s in the ‘U’ where your index finger curves into your thumb,” he said, showing me. “It’s the way you hold things there.”

“在你食指弯向大拇指形成的那个‘U’里，”他边说边比给我看，“就是你拿东西时那儿的姿势。”

My father is holding his hands out above me. His fingers are curled, and his thumbs are pointing at each other, like handles on a bike. I reach my girl arms up straight, and I grasp each of his thumbs as he lifts me off the ground. I hang on, delighted, until my strength is spent. When I think of my father’s hands, I also see them up on stage, in front of thousands in Omaha every year. 想到父亲的手，我还看见它们每年在奥马哈数千人面前的舞台上。他的手指圈着一罐健怡可乐（Diet Coke），捏着花生脆糖或 Dilly Bar 的棍，又或悄悄在喜诗糖果（See's Candies）盒里翻找，瞄准那酒心牛轧糖。他双手抱在身前，摇着头说：“我没什么要补充的。”要不就随着一段更长的哲理回答打起节拍，让体育场里所有的手一起鼓掌。

父亲的手，伴随着每一个绘声绘色的笑话、每一个循循善诱的故事比划着，像雕塑家的手一样确凿地塑造了我。对于父亲的手落在我的手里、落在我儿子的手里，我唯有欣喜与感激。

From Barry Munger

巴里·芒格 撰

Several years ago, I came across a book by Calvin Trillin called Messages from My Father, a memoir about Trillin’s father, Abe, who was born in Ukraine, grew up in Missouri, and spent much of his career operating neighborhood grocery stores in Kansas City. Abe Trillin regarded thrift as a moral virtue, paid his bills the day they arrived, and got up at 4 in the morning, six days a week, to pick the produce for his stores. A man of few words, he was nevertheless convivial, trenchantly funny, and spoke naturally to small children. He was skilled at cards. He was sardonic but had an underlying optimism that one could get along in the world with the proper outlook and character.

几年前，我偶然读到卡尔文·特里林（Calvin Trillin）写的一本书《父亲的来信》（Messages from My Father），一本关于他父亲艾比（Abe）的回忆录。艾比生于乌克兰（Ukraine），在密苏里州（Missouri）长大，职业生涯大半用来打理堪萨斯城（Kansas City）几家社区杂货店。艾比·特里林把节俭视为一种美德，账单一到当天就付，每周六天、凌晨四点起床，去为店里挑菜。他话不多，却很随和、犀利风趣，跟小孩子说话也自然。他牌技了得。他爱讽刺，骨子里却乐观，相信只要有正确的见识和品格，人就能在这世上过得好。

The fact that my father shares many of these qualities, even if he’s not known for his discernment about produce, does not fully explain my attachment to this light, deft, and anecdotal little book. Reading it somehow conjures my father for me, even though in the broad outlines of his life my father has almost nothing in common with Abe Trillin, other than the fact that my father once worked part-time at a Midwestern grocery store, Buffett & Son in Omaha.

父亲虽不以会挑菜闻名，却具备艾比诸多品质，但这并不能完全解释我为何钟情这本轻快、灵巧、满是轶事的小书。读它不知怎的让我想起父亲——尽管在人生的大轮廓上，父亲和艾比·特里林几乎毫无共同之处，除了一点：父亲曾在奥马哈一家中西部的杂货店“巴菲特父子”（Buffett and Son）打过兼职。

Like my father, Abe Trillin had a fundamental reserve, partially Midwestern in origin, that was at odds with his personable qualities. He did not regard a long drive in a car or a fishing outing as an opportunity to catch up. He did not linger on the telephone. His son eventually came to marvel at “how much my father managed to get across to me without those heart-to-hearts that I’ve read about fathers and sons having in the study or in the rowboat or in the car.” The title Messages from My Father comes from the author’s surmise that his father must have been communicating his expectations through coded messages. “It’s possible that my father had a code so subtle that I didn’t know of its existence,” he writes.

和父亲一样，艾比·特里林骨子里有一种矜持，部分源自中西部，与他随和的性情格格不入。他不把长途开车或钓鱼出行当作“叙旧”的机会，也不爱在电话上磨蹭。他儿子最终惊叹于“我父亲竟能在没有那些我所读到的父子间促膝长谈——在书房、在划艇、在车里——的情况下，把那么多东西传达给我”。《父亲的来信》这个书名，来自作者一个猜想：父亲必定是通过某种加密的信息在传达他的期望。“也许我父亲用的密码微妙到我根本不知道它的存在，”他写道。

Anyone who knows my father knows that his manner of expression is not always subtle, but he has many ways of sending his messages. If he doesn’t like the way his bridge partner plays out a hand, for example, he might say, “You played that like a plumber,” but if he wants to offer serious counsel to one of his children, he is more likely to couch the message in an anecdote, preferably delivered in a group setting so that no one is singled out. In both instances, he appears blunt and avuncular—that inimitable Charlie—but at the card table,48Card table牌桌 he uses a lack of indirection for harmless ribbing, and at the dinner table,49Dinner table餐桌 he uses indirection to spare feelings. He’s more subtle than he appears.

认识父亲的人都知道，他的表达方式并不总是含蓄，可他传达信息的方式多得很。比如打桥牌时他若看不惯对家出牌，可能会说“你那手牌打得像个水管工”；但要是想给哪个孩子郑重提点，他更可能把意思裹进一个故事里，最好当着一群人的面讲，好不显得是单挑某人。两种情形下，他都显得直率又慈和——那独一无二的查理——但在牌桌上，他用不绕弯子来善意地打趣；在餐桌上，他用绕弯子来顾及别人的感受。他比看上去更微妙。

A friend of mine recently began an anecdote about my father by saying, “So your dad’s sitting in his chair, like Rushmore…” I knew exactly what he meant. 我有个朋友最近讲起父亲，开口便是：“你爸就坐在椅子上，像拉什莫尔山（Rushmore）……”我立刻懂他的意思。没几个人只是往软垫椅上一坐，就能让人想起那座5700英尺的花岗岩山和四位总统的面孔，可我父亲能。芒格（Munger）家的孩子们都曾不同时地向这座拉什莫尔山提出请求，感觉像多萝西（Dorothy）走向奥兹（Oz），只不过奥兹更爱说话。拉什莫尔山并非有求必应。有时父亲从喉头附近发出低沉持续的声音，仿佛拉什莫尔山火山爆发了，却不太好懂。你能比沉默更微妙吗？

When I finished the Trillin book, I sent it to my father. Even if he didn’t recognize himself in it, I figured he would enjoy the book’s Midwestern milieu, the immigrant striving of the Trillin family, and the humor. The book is written with so much affection that I thought I could even use it to communicate such feelings to my dad indirectly, which is the preferred route. At the very least, I thought the book might reassure my father that his messages were being received, even if they were not always heeded.

读完特里林那本书，我把它寄给了父亲。就算他在书里认不出自己，我想他也会喜欢书中中西部的生活氛围、特里林一家的移民奋斗，还有那份幽默。书写得满是深情，我想我甚至能借它把这样的情感间接传达给爸爸——这本就是更可取的方式。至少，我想这本书能让父亲安心：他的信息收到了，尽管未必总被放在心上。

About a week later, the book came back, in a padded envelope, with an address label supplied by his secretary. There was no note, so I wasn’t sure whether he had read the book or rejected it. It seemed untouched, so I concluded that my message had gone unreceived, loose pages tossed on Rushmore. Not much escapes my father, however. It turned out that he had simply instructed his secretary to send copies to the whole family.

大约一周后，书退回来了，装在加垫信封里，地址标签是他秘书贴的。没有便条，所以我不确定他是读了还是拒收了。书看上去没动过，我便断定我的信息石沉大海，散页扔在了拉什莫尔山上。不过，没什么能逃过父亲的眼睛。结果是他不过吩咐秘书给全家人各寄一本。

From Philip Munger

Some of my most affectionate memories of my father are of shopping for clothes at Brooks Brothers and Marks & Spencer. Most people already know that Father is not a big fashion man. He once said that he was nonconformist enough in his behavior and opinions that it made sense to chart a very straight course in attire. His going along with normal social customs and his sense of humor, he said, were what allowed his otherwise sometimes prickly temperament to harmonize with other people.

关于父亲，我最温馨的回忆里，有些是去布鲁克斯兄弟（Brooks Brothers）和马莎百货（Marks and Spencer）买衣服。多数人大抵知道，父亲不是个讲究时髦的人。他曾经说，自己的行为和见解已经够不合群了，所以在穿着上走直路才合理。他说，他顺着寻常社会习俗、又有一份幽默感，正让自己本有时尖刻的脾气能和旁人合得来。

“He once said that he was nonconformist enough in his behavior and opinions that it made sense to chart a very straight course in attire.”他曾说，自己在行为和见解上都足够不循常规，因此衣着上走一条极笔直的路线反倒说得通。

I vividly recall going with my father to Brooks Brothers, when it was still housed in that beautiful old wood-paneled building in downtown Los Angeles, to buy my first serious suit. I must have been about 11 or 12. I can see those polished brass elevator doors opening. We looked through the racks. Father picked out a pinstriped, charcoal gray suit. When I was 16, we went to buy another suit, this time a three-piece, which I wore religiously during my debate days. It kept the icy wind blowing off the lake at Northwestern during a tournament at bay. We bought, at the same time, a pair of wingtip shoes for my summer stint at The Daily Journal (a coming-of-age ceremony required by Father for each boy), shoes which have lasted to this day. There is another theme here. When we bought a brown tweed coat at Marks & Spencer in London, Father said, “This will always keep its crease.” He admired both stores because they were durable institutions and because their merchandise was too, and fairly priced. Durability has always been a first-rate virtue in my father’s view, along with ritual and tradition. He never had a desire to change his primary habits, sartorial or otherwise, once he had, like Franklin, acquired them.

我清楚地记得和父亲去布鲁克斯兄弟买我第一套正装——那时它还在洛杉矶（Los Angeles）市中心那座漂亮的木板老楼里营业。我当时大约十一二岁。我眼前还能看见那两扇擦得锃亮的黄铜电梯门打开。我们在衣架间翻看。父亲挑了一套细条纹、炭灰色的西装。我十六岁那年，我们又去买了一套，这次是三件套，辩论队那些年我雷打不动地穿它。巡回赛时，它替我挡住了西北大学（Northwestern）湖上吹来的刺骨寒风。同时我们还买了一双翼尖鞋，给我暑假到《每日日报》（The Daily Journal）实习穿——那是父亲要求每个男孩都要经历的成年礼——这双鞋至今还在。这里还有另一层意味。我们在伦敦的马莎百货买一件棕色粗花呢外套时，父亲说：“这件永远会挺括有型。”他欣赏这两家店，因为它们都是经久的老字号，货品也一样耐用、定价公道。在父亲眼里，经久耐用向来是一等美德，与仪式和传统并列。一旦像富兰克林那样养成了主要习惯，无论衣着还是其他，他从不打算改。

I still shop at Brooks, partly because each year at Christmas Father gives every child a gift card, which is perfectly timed for the winter sale. But I always end up going more often than that. One year, I used his largesse to purchase trousers with pleats. My father looked at them askance and said, “Do you want to look like a jazz drummer?”

我至今仍在布鲁克斯买衣服，部分因为每年圣诞节父亲给每个孩子一张礼品卡，正好赶上冬季打折。可我总归去得比那更勤。有一年，我用他给的慷慨买了条带褶的西裤。父亲斜眼看了看，说：“你想看上去像个爵士鼓手吗？”

In New York, Brooks is still housed in its grand old building. I think of my father every time I go; I’m very attached to the place. When I went to study at Oxford, in winter 1988, he gave me an old Brooks coat of his, dating from the ’40s, of a sort of tannish-olive hue, I think, with a warm zip-in lining. As I walked home from the Bodleian Library each night, that nasty, damp, penetrating English cold would not get through. When I returned to the United States, I realized I had left the coat on a bus. I wept at the loss. Even now I wish I had that coat.

在纽约，布鲁克斯仍坐落在那座宏伟的老楼里。我每次去都会想起父亲；我对那地方很有感情。1988年冬天我去牛津（Oxford）求学，他送了我一件他旧的布鲁克斯外套，大概是四十年代的，一种黄褐偏橄榄的色调，配着温暖的拉链式内衬。每晚我从博德利图书馆（Bodleian Library）走回家，那恼人、潮湿、刺骨的英伦寒气都透不进来。回到美国后，我才发觉把外套落在了公交车上。我为这丢失哭了。至今我仍希望自己还有那件外套。
